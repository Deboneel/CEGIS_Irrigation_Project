District-level thematic mapping · Rabi 2023–24

Bangladesh irrigates 72% of its cultivated land. The headline hides a fracture this atlas maps district by district.

Rain carries the Aman season. The dry Rabi season — November to March — runs almost entirely on pumped water, and how well each district manages that supply decides national food security. Five layers trace where the water comes from, what powers it, and how much of the aquifer is left.

6.97m
average national groundwater depth — the number this whole atlas is quietly circling back to.
Why this matters

A national average hides who has water, who pays more to pump it, and who's drawing the aquifer down to get it.

A 72% national irrigation rate says almost nothing about sustainability, access, cost, or which regions are quietly being left behind. This atlas maps five variables — irrigation coverage, surface water reliance, groundwater reliance, power source, and groundwater depth — across all 64 districts of Bangladesh, prepared in ArcGIS Pro from BADC survey data.

Irrigation rate by division

% of net cultivated area under irrigation — hover a bar

Layer 02

Surface water–irrigated area

Choropleth, single field (Surface Water Irrigated Area), 7 manual classes, dark purple to yellow. Canals, rivers and low-lift pumps drawing from surface sources — mapped across all 64 districts.

Area share by size class

1,617K ha total — hover a segment
  • The top class (53,000–149,818 ha) holds only 9 districts but covers 42% of all surface-irrigated land.
  • Sunamganj, Sylhet, Gopalganj and the Barisal belt are the largest users — active floodplains with perennial river access.
  • The northwest shows very low surface-water use, not from less irrigation overall, but because groundwater via tube wells replaced surface sources decades ago.
  • Hill tracts are also low — rivers there are fast-flowing and unsuitable for low-lift pumping.
A geography story Where rivers are slow, wide and seasonally full — the south and northeast — surface irrigation works well. 1,617K ha sounds large but is only 28% of total irrigated area: groundwater dominates nationally.
Layer 03

Groundwater–irrigated area

Choropleth, single field, 7 classes, light-to-dark blue gradient. The contrast here is sharper than the surface water map — groundwater is the primary driver of irrigation geography in Bangladesh.

Surface vs. groundwater reliance

national split, 5,778K ha total irrigated area
  • The "Very Large" class (130,000–254,178 ha) — 9 districts — covers 40.3% of all groundwater irrigation; the bottom 9 districts contribute just 0.1%.
  • Northwest concentration is structural: shallow water table (3–7m), flat alluvial plain, decades of STW/DTW investment make extraction cheap and reliable.
  • Southern districts (Barisal, Khulna coast) show near-zero groundwater use — saline intrusion makes extracted water unsuitable for crops.
The system's load-bearing layer At 4,161K ha, groundwater carries 72% of total irrigated area — geographically uneven, and the central risk this atlas tracks: the districts with 99% irrigation rates are drawing the aquifer down every year.
Layer 04

Irrigation by diesel & electric power

Pie symbology per district across two fields — Electricity Area and Diesel Area — with division-level reliance mapped alongside.

Diesel vs. electric share, by division

sorted by electric share — hover a row
  • Diesel still narrowly leads nationally (53.5% vs 46.5%); 43 districts (67%) are diesel-dominant, only 21 electricity-dominant.
  • Rajshahi leads electricity adoption at 64.2% — mature rural electrification and decades of grid connections.
  • Barisal is most diesel-dependent at 87.8% — poor grid connectivity in coastal chars. Sylhet follows at 82.8%.
An economic liability, not a technical one Diesel dominance in two-thirds of districts costs farmers more per hectare and emits more carbon. Rajshahi's electric share proves the transition is achievable with grid investment.

Average max. depth by division

metres below ground level — click a legend chip above to filter
  • National average depth 6.97m, median 6.32m — most upazilas remain in the safe zone.
  • Rajshahi and Mymensingh average over 10m as divisional means — already moderate-to-deep on average.

This is current depletion — not a future risk.

At 4,161K ha, groundwater carries 72% of national irrigation, and the northwest's near-complete coverage was built almost entirely on it. Cross-referencing Layer 03 against Layer 05 confirms the same districts pumping hardest — Rajshahi, parts of Mymensingh and Dhaka division — already sit in moderate-to-deep zoning territory as divisional averages, meaning individual hotspot upazilas run considerably worse.

Eight upazilas already average over 20m depth, with a peak average of 31.45m. Without extraction limits or recharge intervention, the northwest's 99% irrigation rate becomes its own biggest vulnerability — the resource it depends on most is the one it's drawing down fastest.

Conclusion

The system works — but its foundation is narrower than the 72% headline suggests.

Bangladesh irrigates 72% of its net cultivated land during Rabi season — an achievement built almost entirely on groundwater in the northwest, where Rajshahi, Rangpur and Mymensingh carry the system. The south sits at the opposite end, limited by salinity and terrain that tube wells cannot fix. Three gaps need attention next:

Surface water

2.25M non-irrigated hectares, concentrated south

Salinity and tidal influence rule out tube wells across the coastal belt — surface-water and managed-flooding strategies are the only realistic path to closing this gap.

Groundwater

Regulation in the northwest, before extraction outruns recharge

Eight upazilas already average over 20m depth. The districts with the highest irrigation success are the same ones drawing the aquifer down annually.

Power source

Diesel-to-electric transition, south and Sylhet

67% of districts remain diesel-dominant. Rajshahi shows what grid investment can do; closing this gap directly cuts per-hectare costs for smallholder farmers.
